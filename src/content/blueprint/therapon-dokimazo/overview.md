Every government needs people to run it. The question that separates AreTéCracy from every representative system that preceded it is not *who* governs, but *how they got there* — and what keeps them from becoming the thing they were selected to prevent.

**Therapon** is an ancient Greek word meaning a skilled attendant — someone who serves with competence, voluntarily, in genuine service of another. It is the title every civic servant in AreTéCracy holds, from the most junior administrator to the highest magistrate. The name is a statement of architecture: the polis does not serve its servants. Its servants serve it.

**Dokimazo** — *to test, to prove, to temper* — names the examination process that determines whether a citizen is qualified to serve as a Therapon. Athens used *dokimazía* as a final check on candidates already chosen. AreTéCracy inverts the sequence entirely: examination happens before selection, so that whenever the kleroterion draws a name, qualification has already been established at every stage of the pipeline. By the time chance makes its choice, there are no losing slots.

The pipeline on this map moves from left to right. A citizen volunteers; their record is examined anonymously; the best candidates are shaped into a Brillianteer Pool; the Ekklesia confirms the masterstone standard; chance makes the selection; a mandate is written and accepted under oath. What happens after the vow — accountability, reporting, and the terminal evaluation at the close of every term — is the architecture that keeps the gate closed long after the selection ceremony is over.
