**Because it is not Latin.**

That is the short answer, and it is the complete one. Every other explanation follows from it.

The legal and governmental vocabulary of modern republics — *constitution*, *senate*, *veto*, *republic*, *citizen*, *suffrage*, even the word *democracy* as it is commonly misused — descends from Latin. That is not an accident. Latin was the language of Rome, and Rome's contribution to governance was not democracy. It was the Polybian mixed constitution: a system of competing powers, deliberately engineered to keep any one faction from dominating. The senate, the consuls, the tribunes — each a check on the others. Sophisticated, durable, and built, at its foundation, to prevent the people from ever ruling directly.

Polybius called it the ideal constitution. Madison agreed. In Federalist No. 55, he made his position plain: *"Had every Athenian been a Socrates, the Ecclesia would still have been a mob."* He was not describing a flaw in implementation. He was describing a flaw in the concept — the concept that ordinary people, given direct authority, would govern wisely. He feared actual democracy, and what he built reflects that fear. What we call democracy in the modern world is, with respect, aristocracy with better branding. It is the Polybian model. It is Latin.

AreTéCracy is not that.

---

Greek carries a different tradition — not of conquest and administration, but of the *polis* and the *demos*. Words like *philotimo*, *isegoria*, *eudaimonia*, *isonomia*, and *demokratia* are not decorative. They name concepts that have no clean Latin equivalent because the Roman tradition did not contain them. *Philotimo* — love of honor, the civic pride of someone who does right by their community not for reward but because they love being honorable — has no Latin translation that preserves its meaning. *Isegoria* — the equal right of every citizen to speak in the assembly — describes something the Roman senate was specifically designed to prevent.

These are not aesthetic choices. They are political ones. When AreTéCracy uses Greek terminology, it is not reaching for classical flavor. It is reaching for a tradition that is philosophically incompatible with the Polybian model — a tradition that starts from the premise that the people are sovereign, not as a slogan, but as a structural fact.

---

Athens got some things badly wrong. It executed Socrates. It excluded women, slaves, and foreigners. It made catastrophic military decisions. And then Philip of Macedon defeated it at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, and the experiment was interrupted by force. We have studied those failures, and the architecture of AreTéCracy is built in part to correct them.

But there is a second reason Athenian democracy did not spread: it could not scale. The Attic peninsula is small. If you lived at Sounion, the farthest point of Attica, you could walk to the Pnyx in Athens in a day or two. Every eligible citizen could actually show up. For the American founders in 1787, the geography was an entirely different problem. Getting from Savannah to Philadelphia was a matter of weeks, not days — by sea, subject to weather and Atlantic storms; by land, to carnivorous animals, hostile territory, and highway robbers along stretches of the route. Direct democracy across that distance was not a philosophical question. It was physically impossible. Representative democracy was not only Madison's preference. Given the realities of 1787, it was the only option that did not require citizens to risk their lives to participate.

Until now.

---

In this decade — in the last few years specifically — artificial intelligence has crossed a threshold. Not as a novelty, and not as the private instrument of those funding it. As a tool capable of doing what no human bureaucracy ever could: serving as a *therapon* — a skilled servant working in genuine service of the Ekklesia, the people as a whole. Translating the will of millions into actionable governance. Surfacing what is known, flagging what is disputed, executing what is decided, and doing all of it transparently, at scale, in real time.

The scalability problem that made Athenian democracy a local phenomenon is solved. The tools that make direct, supermajority-based governance possible at a national scale exist right now. The only thing missing is the will to use them differently.

That is what AreTéCracy is for.

The Greek is here to say: the experiment was interrupted. It was never finished. It was never proven impossible.

And the interruption is over.
